Schengen Family & Friend Visit Visa

What this covers

Who a visit application is for

A short-stay Type C Schengen visa for visiting family or friends who live in the Schengen area, and staying in their home rather than in a hotel. It permits up to 90 days in any rolling 180-day period across the area as a whole.

It is not the same application as tourism, even though people often submit it as one. The moment your accommodation is somebody's home rather than a booking, the consulate's question changes: instead of asking whether you have somewhere to stay, it asks who this person is, how they know you, whether they really live there, and who is paying. Those questions are answered by documents your host provides, not by you.

If the person you are visiting is an EU, EEA or Swiss citizen and you are their spouse, partner or close family member, you may fall under a quite different and considerably more favourable route — the visa should be free of charge and handled under an accelerated procedure. See the EU family member route before you use this one.

If you hold a British passport you do not need this. This page is for non-UK nationals living in Britain, whose need for a visa is decided by their passport rather than their UK status.

Your host's half

What your host has to provide

This is where visit applications stall, and it is rarely the applicant's fault. Your host is being asked for documents that feel intrusive, on a deadline they did not set, for a process they do not understand.

Several Schengen countries require a formal, official hospitality declaration rather than a simple letter. It goes by a different name in each: a dichiarazione di ospitalità in Italy, a Verpflichtungserklärung in Germany, a bewijs van garantstelling in the Netherlands. These are not letters your host writes at the kitchen table. They are official forms, obtained and in some cases certified through a local authority in the host's own country, and they take time to get. Which of them applies depends on where your host lives, and it must be established before the appointment rather than discovered at the counter.

Alongside that, your host will generally need to provide:

An invitation letterWho they are, who you are, how you know each other, the exact dates, and which costs they are covering
A copy of their identity documentPassport, national identity card, or residence permit if they are not a national of the country they live in

What sponsoring means

What sponsoring you actually commits your host to

Nobody explains this to hosts, and they should be told before they sign anything.

A formal hospitality or sponsorship declaration is a legal undertaking, not a courtesy. In several countries it makes the host financially liable for the visitor's accommodation, subsistence, medical costs and, if it comes to it, the costs of removal, for a defined period. That liability sits with them, is enforceable, and does not end when the visit does.

It also means their own documents get read. Their income, their housing, their immigration status if they are not a national of the country they live in. Hosts who agree quickly, without realising this, sometimes withdraw halfway through, which is a worse outcome for everyone than an honest conversation at the start.

So have the conversation first. If your host is not comfortable taking on a financial undertaking, that is entirely legitimate, and there is usually an alternative: you fund the trip yourself and evidence it from your own account, with your host providing accommodation and an invitation but no financial guarantee. That is a perfectly ordinary file. What does not work is a file that is vague about which of the two arrangements applies.

Your half

What you have to provide

The core of every short-stay application is the same:

DocumentWhat the consulate is checking
Completed and signed application formThat it agrees with every other document in the file
PassportValid at least three months beyond your departure from the Schengen area, issued within the last ten years, with two blank pages
Two recent photographsTo the Schengen biometric specification, not a passport photo from three years ago
Proof of UK immigration statusA share code, eVisa or residence permit showing you live here lawfully and for long enough to return
Travel medical insuranceMinimum EUR 30,000, valid in all Schengen states, covering the whole stay including repatriation
Proof of accommodationEvery night of the stay accounted for
Proof of onward or return travelThat you have a route home and a reason to take it
Financial evidenceUsually bank statements with a settled history, sufficient for the trip you have described

With the accommodation row satisfied by your host's invitation and declaration rather than a hotel booking, two things carry extra weight in a visit application.

Evidence of the relationship. Officers see invented invitations. A stated relationship with nothing behind it is weak, and it is easily strengthened: family certificates for relatives, and for friends, the ordinary trail of a real friendship — photographs together, correspondence over time, evidence of a previous visit in either direction. It feels odd to submit and it is worth submitting.

Evidence that you will come back. Visit applications attract more scrutiny here than tourist ones, because a stay in a family home has no checkout date pushing you out. Employment, study, a tenancy, dependants, a business — whatever ties you to Britain belongs in the file, made explicit rather than left to be inferred. A cover letter is where the relationship and the reason for returning are drawn together into a single readable account.

What it costs

What a visit visa costs

The official Schengen visa fee is EUR 90 for an adult and EUR 45 for a child aged six to eleven. Children under six are free. It is set by the European Union, is the same for every Schengen country, and is not refunded if the application is refused.

Note that a formal hospitality declaration frequently carries its own administrative fee in the host's country, payable by them to their local authority. It is not part of the visa fee and it is not something we handle.

If you are the family member of an EU citizen exercising free movement, you should not be paying the EUR 90 at all. That route is set out separately, and it is worth checking before you pay.

Honest limits

How we help, and what we cannot do

What we do.

We establish which country's rules apply to your host and whether a formal declaration is needed, which is the question that most often derails a visit application. We tell your host exactly what is required, in plain terms, including what sponsoring commits them to. We handle the consulate question and the appointment, build both halves of the file, cross-reference the dates, draft the cover letter and audit before submission.

What we cannot do.

We cannot guarantee, expedite or influence a decision. We are not a consulate, we are not a visa application centre, and we hold no authorisation from any government. We cannot create appointment slots that do not exist. The consulate decides; we prepare the file.

When you do not need us.

Plenty of applications are straightforward enough to do yourself, and we will tell you when yours is one of them rather than take a fee for work that does not need doing.

One limit specific to this application.

We can tell your host what is needed and why, but we cannot obtain a hospitality declaration for them — it is issued to them, by an authority in their country, on their signature. If your host will not or cannot complete it, no amount of work on our side substitutes for it, and we will tell you that plainly rather than take a fee to try.
