Who this is for

Do you actually need a German visa?

If you hold a British passport, no. The German missions state it plainly: British citizens do not require a visa for the Schengen member states, provided the stay does not exceed 90 days in any 180-day period and no economic activity is pursued. From October 2026 you will need an ETIAS travel authorisation, which is applied for online and is not a visa.

If you are a non-UK national living in Britain, it depends on your passport, not your UK status. If your nationality requires a Schengen visa, you need one, and indefinite leave to remain, a work visa, a student visa or an eVisa does not exempt you.

Germany is unusually explicit about residence. The missions state that they will only accept visa applications from UK residents, including foreign residents holding a UK residence permit, a valid share code or a UKVI account. Applications from people who are not resident here are considered only in very exceptional cases, and at the ultimate discretion of a visa officer. If you are in Britain on a short visit rather than living here, Germany is not a consulate you can simply choose.

One more distinction. Everything below concerns the short-stay Schengen visa, up to 90 days. If you are moving to Germany to work, study or join family, that is a German national visa, applied for through a different route under different rules, and not what we handle.

Three separate costs

What a Germany visa costs from the UK

The official Schengen visa fee is EUR 90 for an adult and EUR 45 for a child aged six to eleven. Children under six are free. The German missions confirm the EUR 90 figure for Schengen A and C visas, and state that fees are not reimbursed if an application is denied.

How you pay matters here more than it does elsewhere. The German missions accept Mastercard or Visa only, the named cardholder must be present to sign the voucher, and digital wallets are not accepted. The route

TLScontact, not VFS: how a German application works

This is the detail most guides get wrong, and it costs people appointments. Germany does not use VFS Global in the United Kingdom. The German missions state that Schengen type C visas can only be applied for through their external service provider, TLScontact. Booking is done at the TLScontact platform, not through a VFS account.

If you have read elsewhere that you apply for a German visa through VFS in London, that guidance is wrong, and following it will send you to the wrong provider entirely.

TLScontact operates three centres:

CentreWho it servesWhich mission decides
LondonResidents of England, Wales and Northern IrelandThe German Embassy in London

The missions publish a consular district map, and there are exceptions within England. Check yours before you book rather than after.

TLScontact collects the file and the biometrics. It does not assess the application and it cannot influence the outcome. The decision is the mission's alone.

The queue

The appointment waiting list, and why it exists

Since 1 June 2026 the German Embassy has run appointment waiting lists for some C-visa categories. The stated reasons are two: demand for appointments exceeds the visa section's capacity, and the Embassy wants to protect applicants from what it describes as fraudulent and expensive appointment booking agencies.

The mechanism matters. You register for a waiting list rather than picking a slot from a calendar. The Embassy warns that you must register for the correct waiting list and book your appointment in the correct appointment category, or you risk being turned away. Registering on the wrong list is not a recoverable mistake on the day; it is a wasted place in a queue you then have to rejoin.

Two consequences follow for anybody planning a trip to Germany. The first is that your travel dates cannot be fixed before your appointment is. The second is that the categories are not interchangeable, and choosing between them is a judgement about your trip, not a form-filling detail.

It is also worth naming the thing the Embassy is warning about. Agencies that sell appointment slots are selling something they do not own. We do not sell slots. We tell you exactly which list and category are correct for your trip and what the official registration asks of you — and if the queue means your intended dates are not achievable, we tell you that instead of taking the fee.

Working days, not calendar days

Why Germany's fifteen days are longer than everyone else's

The standard Schengen figure, quoted everywhere including by us, is fifteen calendar days for a decision, extendable to forty-five where additional checks apply.

Germany does not quote it that way. The missions state that the processing time for a Schengen visa is approximately fifteen working days from the date the application is received by the Embassy or the Consulate General, and that a further two to three days should be allowed for the passport to come back.

Fifteen working days is three calendar weeks before you count bank holidays. Fifteen calendar days is a fortnight and a day. On a tight timeline that difference is the whole margin, and it is invisible to anybody reading a generic Schengen guide.

Two further points. The clock starts when the mission receives the file, not when you attend TLScontact. And processing is extended to forty-five days for nationals of the Russian Federation, The Gambia, Somalia and Guinea, following the suspension of visa facilitation agreements. If you hold one of those passports, plan against forty-five days rather than fifteen.

More than one country

Applying to Germany when your trip covers more than one country

A Schengen visa issued by Germany is valid across all twenty-nine Schengen states. Whether Germany is entitled to issue it is a separate question.

You apply to the country where you will spend the most nights. Where the nights are split equally, you apply to the country you enter first. Frankfurt as a connection does not make Germany your main destination, and a two-night stop in Berlin on a trip that is otherwise Italy does not either.

Germany is a common wrong answer here precisely because it is a common transit point. A change of planes in Frankfurt or Munich, airside, is not an entry to Germany at all. If you are leaving the airside area, or your itinerary genuinely centres on Germany, that is different.

Where files fail

Where German applications run into trouble

The wrong provider.

Covered above, and it remains the most avoidable error on this page.

The wrong waiting list or appointment category.

Also covered above, and the Embassy has said in terms what happens.

A timeline built on fifteen calendar days.

People book flights against the generic Schengen figure, then discover the German figure is quoted in working days and that the passport return sits on top of it.

Proof of UK residence that does not satisfy the requirement.

Germany is explicit that it accepts applications from UK residents. A share code that has expired, or a UKVI account you cannot access on the day, is a real problem rather than a formality.

Payment that cannot be taken.

Mastercard or Visa, named cardholder present. No digital wallets.

A file that is internally inconsistent.

The dates on the flights, the hotels, the insurance, the employer's letter and the form all have to agree with each other. They routinely do not, because they were gathered over several weeks. This is the most common thing we correct, and it is why the audit sits before submission.

How we can help, and what we cannot do

What we do.

We establish whether Germany is the correct consulate for your itinerary, and say so if it is not. We confirm the correct TLScontact waiting list and category for your case, and tell you exactly how the registration works. We build the document set, cross-reference the dates, draft the cover letter, review the form, and audit the file before submission. We plan the timeline against Germany's working-day figure rather than the generic one.

What we cannot do.

We cannot guarantee, expedite or influence a decision. We are not the Embassy, we are not TLScontact, and we hold no authorisation from either. We cannot move you up a waiting list, and we cannot create appointments. The Embassy has publicly warned about people who claim they can.

When you do not need us.

A straightforward single-country trip to Germany, with a stable job, a clean travel history and three months in hand, is an application many people complete themselves. We will tell you so. Where the fee earns itself is a split itinerary, a previous refusal, a tight timeline, a complex sponsor, or self-employed income that needs setting out properly.
